Search stopped being a list. It became an answer. That single change breaks most
of what content teams were built to do, and the response so far has mostly been
to do the old thing faster.

This is the sequence I use instead. Four stages, in order, every time. The
stages don't change. What they produce changes completely, because the answer
comes out of your company rather than out of a framework.

## The room got too loud, so people stopped listening

Every company in every category now publishes. The tooling got cheap, then it
got free, and the volume went up accordingly. The standard response to a crowded
channel has been to add more: more posts, more formats, more distribution, on
the theory that presence is a numbers game.

It isn't, and it stopped being one a while ago. The room is loud enough now that
volume is indistinguishable from noise. Noise is the thing buyers are actively
filtering out, and they have better filters than they used to.

What they're doing instead is narrowing. They ask one specific question and they
want one good answer. Increasingly they ask a model, and the model returns a
synthesis with a handful of citations attached. There is no page two. There is
often no page one.

That behavior is bad for anyone competing on output. It is very good for anyone
who can be the definitive answer to a narrow question.

## Why playbooks don't work here

A playbook is a set of decisions made in advance, by someone who has not seen
your data. It travels well and it applies badly. The reason it survives is that
it can be sold repeatedly with the client's logo swapped in.

Answer engine optimization, or AEO — the practice of getting your company cited
in AI-generated answers rather than ranked in a list of links — is a bad fit for
that model. The work is downstream of specifics. Which questions your buyers ask
in their own words. Which sources the models pull from when they answer those
questions. Whether your site is legible to a crawler at all. Which of your
competitors already owns the sentence you want. None of that is knowable in
advance, and all of it changes what you should do first.

So the method is a sequence, not a template. The order is the part that
transfers. The content of each stage is yours.

## 01 · Listen

The first stage finds out what is actually being asked, and who currently gets
to answer it.

That means the real query set, in the words your buyers use, not the words your
category page uses. It comes from sales calls, support tickets, churn interviews,
and the questions your team fields so often they have stopped noticing them.
Those questions are usually the most valuable thing in the building and nobody
has written them down.

It also means a baseline. I run a fixed set of prompts against the major
assistants and record what comes back: whether you appear, in what position,
described how, and which sources the answer was built from. That last part
matters more than the rest. Models cite what they trust, and the list of what
they trust is short, specific, and mostly not your website.

The stage ends when you know the questions and you know who owns them today.

## 02 · Locate

The second stage decides what position you can plausibly own within a year.

Plausibly is doing real work in that sentence. Most positioning exercises pick
the position the company wants. This one picks the position the company can hold
against the competitors who are already there, given the surfaces the answers
come from and the time available.

Sometimes the position exists and nobody has claimed it, which is the easy case
and rarer than you would hope. Sometimes it doesn't exist yet, and the work is
naming it. Naming a category is slower and harder than occupying one, and it is
occasionally the only honest option.

The stage ends with a sentence you can defend and a set of questions you intend
to own.

## 03 · Saturate

The third stage gets you into every surface where the question gets asked.

Owned content is the smallest part of this. It is the part everyone starts with
because it is the part you control, and controlling it is not the same as being
found through it.

The larger part is infrastructure and third parties. Infrastructure means
whether a model can read you at all: server-rendered content, clean semantic
markup, structured data that matches what's on the page, no material content
locked behind a click or a script. A site that needs JavaScript to reveal its
own paragraphs is invisible to a lot of what now decides whether you exist.

Third parties means the sources the models actually pull from. Comparison sites,
review platforms, community threads, industry publications, documentation,
directories that nobody thinks about. Your presence in those sources is a larger
input to what a model says about you than your blog is.

The stage ends with a prioritized roadmap, sequenced by impact rather than by
what is easiest to start.

## 04 · Sustain

The fourth stage measures whether any of it worked.

Measurement in AI search is unreliable for a boring reason: the measurement
basis moves. Ask a different question, ask it a different way, ask it a week
later, and the answer changes. Most reporting in this space compares numbers
that were never comparable.

The fix is to fix the basis. A locked prompt set, written down, unchanged
quarter to quarter. A named competitor list. A set cadence. Share of voice —
the proportion of answers to your prompt set in which you appear at all — then
citation position, then sentiment, then which sources drove each result.

Fixed is the operative word.

> A number that isn't comparable quarter over quarter isn't a number. It's a
> mood.

## Categories don't create themselves

The most expensive mistake I see is a company deciding it has created a category
because it has started using a phrase.

A category exists when other people use the word without being prompted. Not
your marketing site. Analysts, competitors, buyers describing their own problem,
job listings, and the models that have read all of it. Until then you have a
tagline, and a tagline is a claim you are making about yourself.

The mechanism is worth being precise about, because it explains why this is
slow. A model's answer about your category is assembled from what it has read.
If the only places using your term are your own properties, the term reads as
proprietary language and gets translated back into the category the rest of the
market uses. You get described as a version of the incumbent. That description
then trains the next answer, and the answer after that.

Getting out of that loop requires the term to appear in sources you don't own,
attached to a definition that is consistent everywhere it appears. That means a
definition short enough to be quoted, published somewhere quotable, and repeated
without being reworded every time someone likes a different adjective. It means
research that gives other people a reason to cite the term while citing the
data. It means the term surviving contact with sales calls, where the buyer
either recognizes the problem it names or doesn't.

Most companies should not try. The correct move for the majority is to win a
narrow, existing question decisively, which is faster and defensible. Naming a
category is right when the existing category actively misdescribes what you do,
and the cost of being misdescribed is larger than the cost of the campaign.

That is a strategy decision, and it should be made deliberately and early. It is
the single most common thing I am hired to argue about.

## What an engagement looks like

Most engagements start with an audit, because I'm not going to tell you what to
do before I know what's true. The audit runs the first two stages: the query
set, the baseline, the technical teardown, the competitive picture, and a
position with a roadmap attached.

After that the work splits by what you already have. If you have people
producing content but nobody setting direction, the work is standing direction:
strategy, editorial standard, measurement, and management of whoever executes.
If you have a plan and dev capacity but nobody to bridge them, the work is
translating the roadmap into tickets an engineering team will prioritize, then
verifying that what shipped did what it was supposed to.

I set strategy and direction. I don't write your posts or build your site. Your
team or your contractors do that, and my job is deciding what should be made,
setting the standard it has to meet, and holding the line on whether it got met.

The first thing I will usually tell you is something you didn't want to hear
about your positioning. That is the value. Everything after it is sequencing.
