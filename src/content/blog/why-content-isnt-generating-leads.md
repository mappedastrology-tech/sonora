## Why isn't my content generating leads?

Content usually fails to generate leads for one of four reasons: it targets questions people ask before they're ready to buy, the company can't be described in one sentence so nothing sticks, the work is published but never distributed, or nobody is measuring anything connected to revenue. Almost every stalled content program is broken at one of those four points, and the fix depends entirely on which one.

Publishing more rarely helps. If the problem is that you're ranking for questions nobody buys from, doubling output doubles the wrong thing.

Here's how to find your actual failure point.

## Are you targeting buying questions or browsing questions?

There is a real difference between someone reading about a problem and someone comparing two vendors, and most content programs only serve the first.

Keyword tools sort by volume, which surfaces the broad top-of-funnel questions — *what is X*, *how does X work*, *X best practices*. Those get traffic. They rarely get deals, because the person asking them is six months from a budget.

The questions that produce pipeline look different. They're comparative (*X vs Y*), they're specific (*X for regulated industries*), they name a competitor, or they carry a constraint (*cheapest X for small teams*). Volume is lower. Intent is enormously higher.

**How to check:** pull your top ten pages by traffic and your top ten pages by conversions. If there's no overlap, you're publishing for an audience that isn't buying.

## Can a stranger describe what you do in one sentence?

If your homepage says one thing, your sales deck says another, and your documentation implies a third, no amount of content fixes it. Five descriptions means no description.

This matters more now than it used to. Your message has to survive being chopped into dozens of pieces, pasted into ChatGPT by someone doing research, and repeated by an internal champion in a meeting you'll never attend. Every one of those requires a short, stable, repeated description of what you are.

A useful test: could a competitor put their logo on your homepage and have the copy still make sense? If yes, you don't have positioning. You have filler, and search engines and AI models have the same problem with it that buyers do — nothing to attach to you.

**How to check:** ask three people on your team who your product is for. If you get three answers, that's the finding.

## Where does the content actually go after you publish it?

Lean teams spend around 90% of their effort creating content and 10% distributing it. It should be closer to even.

Publishing to your own blog and waiting is not a distribution strategy, and it's become a worse one. In early 2026, 68% of US Google searches ended without a single click, and only about 231 of every 1,000 reach the open web at all. You can rank first and receive nothing.

Meanwhile, when AI systems answer a question, the sources they cite are mostly not the vendor's own site. Across more than 25 million cited links from ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini, 84% of citations came from earned media — journalism, research, third-party coverage, and substantial corporate content elsewhere. Paid and advertorial content accounted for 0.3%.

That means three layers have to work at once:

- **Owned** — your site, structured so a model can actually read it. Clean served HTML, structured data, and content that isn't buried behind JavaScript.
- **Earned** — reviews, forums, comparison sites, analyst coverage, press. This is the majority of what gets cited and the layer nobody owns internally.
- **Bottom of funnel** — comparison pages, alternatives pages, clear pricing, and direct answers to the objections your sales calls keep hitting. Ranking for the question that starts a search is worth very little if you're missing from the page where it ends.

**How to check:** search three questions your buyers would ask, in ChatGPT and in Google. Note who gets named. If it isn't you, note where those sources live — that's your distribution gap.

## What are you actually measuring?

Pageviews are not a pipeline metric. Neither are impressions, time on page, or keyword rankings on their own.

The measurement that matters connects specific queries and specific pages to real deals. Which pages appear in closed-won journeys? Which queries bring people who convert? Which content does sales actually send to prospects?

Most teams can't answer those questions, so they optimize for the numbers they can see, and those numbers keep going up while the pipeline stays flat. That's the exact pattern that makes a founder conclude content doesn't work.

**How to check:** name the last three deals that closed. Can you say what content they touched? If not, the reporting isn't wired to anything.

## The four questions in order

Those four checks map to a sequence, and the order matters because each answer depends on the one before it.

**Listen** — learn the market, the competition, and what your buyers actually search when they have a budget and a problem.

**Locate** — find the strongest differentiator you can hold onto for a year, and the language that makes a stranger understand what you sell.

**Saturate** — show up across the whole path, from the question that starts the search to the comparison page where the decision gets made.

**Sustain** — measure against pipeline instead of pageviews.

You can't decide what to publish before you know what you're the answer to. You can't measure whether it worked without knowing what you were aiming at. Teams that run these out of order end up with rising output and flat pipeline, which is where most stalled content programs actually live.

## Common questions

**How long does content take to generate leads?**
Plan on six to twelve months for search-driven content to produce meaningful pipeline. Search compounds slowly, indexing and ranking take time, and changes in AI answers usually surface in retrieval-based engines like Perplexity before appearing elsewhere. Anyone promising results in thirty days is guessing.

**Should I publish more content or better content?**
Neither, until you know which of the four failure points is yours. If you're targeting the wrong queries, more content produces more of the wrong traffic. If your positioning is unclear, better writing won't fix what the writing is about. Diagnose before you produce.

**How much content do I need before it works?**
There's no threshold number. Ten pages aimed at buying-intent questions will outperform a hundred aimed at browsing questions. The variable that matters is whether the content answers what someone asks when they're close to spending money.

**Does SEO still work in 2026?**
Yes, but ranking and traffic have decoupled. Two thirds of searches now end without a click, so being visible in the answer matters as much as being ranked in the list. The technical foundations still apply — they're just no longer sufficient on their own.

**What is answer engine optimization?**
Answer engine optimization, or AEO, is the practice of getting your company named and cited by AI systems that answer questions directly instead of returning links. It covers the technical work that lets a model parse your site, the content structures it extracts answers from, and the third-party sources it cites when describing your category.
